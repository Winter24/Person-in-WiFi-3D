\begin{abstract}
WiFi-based human pose estimation offers an occlusion-resilient alternative to camera-based sensing, but estimating multi-person 3D skeletons from channel state information remains difficult because WiFi signals are noisy, low-dimensional, and strongly affected by multipath propagation. This paper presents a compact draft-to-refine redesign of the Person-in-WiFi 3D pipeline. A controlled architecture ladder separates representation, sequence encoder, pose head, and flow-refinement choices. Under the inherited three-receiver Person-in-WiFi 3D evaluation protocol, Mamba-2 Flow (two steps) achieves 165.487 mm MPJPE: a 7.053 mm (4.09\%) improvement over the PETR reference, while increasing throughput from 118.7 to 209.9 FPS, reducing parameters from 13.130M to 3.618M, and reducing peak allocated memory from 155.60 to 25.29 MB. The result establishes compact sequence modeling with two-step flow refinement as an accuracy-oriented alternative under this WiFi pose-estimation protocol.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
WiFi sensing, CSI, 3D human pose estimation, Mamba2, flow matching, spectral tokenization.

\section{Introduction}
WiFi channel state information (CSI) provides a non-visual sensing signal that can be used for human perception in indoor environments. Unlike camera-based pose estimation, WiFi sensing can operate under occlusion and low-light conditions, but the input is indirect: body motion perturbs multipath propagation rather than producing explicit visual evidence. This makes multi-person 3D pose estimation from WiFi substantially harder than pose estimation from images.

Person-in-WiFi 3D demonstrated an end-to-end PETR-style formulation for multi-person 3D pose estimation from WiFi CSI \cite{yan2024personwifi3d}. We ask whether its pose-decoding path can be made smaller and faster while preserving pose accuracy. The resulting architecture ladder compares spectral tokenization, a PETR query decoder and a lightweight query-pose head, Transformer and Mamba-family encoders, draft-only variants, and one- versus two-step flow refinement. The selected Mamba-2 Flow (two steps) configuration combines spectral tokenization, a flattened Mamba-2 sequence encoder, a lightweight query-pose head, and two-step flow refinement.

The study uses the three-receiver, fixed-layout Person-in-WiFi 3D setup and its temporal train/test split. It therefore evaluates compact architecture choices under a common sensing and evaluation protocol; cross-person and cross-environment generalization are outside the scope of this ablation.

``Flow is All You Need'' describes the final correction stage of this compact architecture, rather than flow in isolation. In the studied setting, lightweight flow refinement complements a compact query-pose head while retaining strong pose accuracy.

Our contribution is a controlled architecture study that jointly reports MPJPE, scene-cardinality results, throughput, parameter count, and peak allocated memory. The ablation identifies Mamba-2 Flow (two steps) as the lowest-MPJPE configuration and Mamba-2 Draft as the throughput and memory endpoint; complementary diagnostics characterize both operating points.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Human Pose from Images}
\label{sec:related-camera}
Camera-based pose estimation is the most mature paradigm and supplies the conceptual templates this paper inherits \cite{zheng2023posesurvey}. Bottom-up methods such as OpenPose \cite{cao2017openpose} regress per-pixel keypoint heatmaps and part affinity fields and then associate them into instances, which scales gracefully with crowd size. Top-down methods such as AlphaPose \cite{fang2017alphapose} first run a person detector and then a single-person estimator, usually attaining higher accuracy at the cost of cropping overhead. High-resolution backbones such as HRNet \cite{sun2019hrnet} sharpen the accuracy ceiling by preserving spatial resolution across the network. These methods motivate the set-prediction perspective we later adopt for WiFi but inherit the deployment limits of cameras---appearance capture, lighting dependence, and reduced reliability under occlusion---which conflict with privacy-sensitive indoor monitoring.

\subsection{Human Pose from Wi-Fi}
Radio and WiFi sensing developed from through-wall pose recovery and ambient physiological or gesture sensing \cite{zhao2018rfpose,adib2015smarthomes,abdelnasser2015wigest,zhang2021widar3}. CSI-based representations then supported person perception and pose recovery without an image stream \cite{wang2018csinet,wang2019canwifi,wang2019personwifi2d,jiang2020wipose}. WiNect and GoPose advanced free-form single-person 3D tracking \cite{ren2021winect,ren2022gopose}, while MM-Fi broadened multimodal wireless benchmarks \cite{yang2023mmfi}. Application-oriented systems have also considered metaverse avatars, joint activity--pose analysis, and industrial skeleton monitoring \cite{yang2022metafi,boudlal2024simultaneous,yin2024powerskel}. Recent graph-based and lightweight continuous-pose studies further illustrate the range of structural and efficiency choices in WiFi pose modeling \cite{chen2025graphwifi,dao2026wiflow}.

Person-in-WiFi 3D adapts the detection-and-regression perspective to WiFi CSI and provides the baseline used in this study \cite{yan2024personwifi3d}. A related short-form study also reports 3D WiFi pose estimation under commodity sensing \cite{ren2021wifi3d}. Transformer architectures became important for structured prediction after the success of attention-based sequence modeling \cite{vaswani2017attention}, while the DETR family of set predictors \cite{carion2020detr} and its pose-estimation extension PETR \cite{shi2022petr} provide the architectural template we modify.

State space models such as Mamba provide linear-time sequence modeling with selective recurrence \cite{gu2023mamba}. Vision Mamba, VMamba, and LocalMamba show how selective state-space computation can be adapted to structured visual tokens \cite{zhu2024visionmamba,liu2024vmamba,huang2024localmamba}. Mamba-2 further connects structured state space models and attention-like computation \cite{dao2024mamba2}. These properties motivate replacing a heavier Transformer encoder with a compact flattened Mamba-2 sequence encoder for WiFi token sequences.

Rectified flow learns a velocity field for transporting samples between source and target distributions \cite{liu2023rectifiedflow}. Diffusion-based 3D pose methods have likewise used iterative generation, multi-hypothesis aggregation, and learned refinement to improve pose structure \cite{gong2023diffpose,shan2023d3dp,kang2024diffusionrefine}. Here, however, the source is not random noise; it is a draft 3D pose produced by the detection head. The flow module is therefore used as a learned pose refinement operator. Because no reflow stage is performed, the interpretation is closer to conditional flow-matching refinement than a full generative rectified-flow pipeline.

Recent WiFi-pose work separates fixed-benchmark pose estimation from robustness-focused protocols: Person-in-WiFi 3D uses a fixed receiver layout with receiver-count analysis, while DT-Pose studies domain-consistent representation learning as a dedicated cross-domain problem \cite{chen2025robustwifi}. We follow the former convention to focus on compact architecture design and application-scale inference.

\section{System and Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:wifi-signals}
\subsection{Channel State Information}
Commodity WiFi devices communicate over $N$ orthogonal subcarriers; for each subcarrier $i$ the received and transmitted symbols are related by
\begin{equation}
    Y_i = H_i X_i + n_i,\quad i \in [1,N],
    \label{eq:csi}
\end{equation}
where $H_i \in \mathbb{C}$ is the channel state information (CSI), $X_i$ the transmitted symbol, and $n_i$ additive noise. Each $H_i$ encodes the joint effect of path loss, scattering, reflection, and small-scale fading along the propagation path, so its amplitude and phase vary with the body geometry, motion, and pose of people between transmitter and receiver. This dependency makes WiFi a viable sensing modality for pose, but it also means the signal aggregates contributions from the whole scene rather than localizing them to a specific person, which is fundamentally harder than visual input.

\subsection{Phase Sanitization}
Raw CSI phase is corrupted by carrier-frequency offset, sampling-frequency offset, and packet detection delay, which together appear as a per-packet linear drift across subcarriers. Standard sanitization techniques fit and remove this drift before the signal is consumed by a learning model \cite{sen2012csiapproach}. We follow the project default and apply discrete-wavelet amplitude denoising together with linear-phase normalization at the dataset stage; this leaves both amplitude and a stabilized phase available to the downstream pipeline. The denoising stage is treated as a fixed preprocessing component and is not retrained.

\subsection{Hardware Configuration}
The sensing layout uses one transmitter and three receivers around a $3.5 \times 4$~m indoor area. Each receiver contributes three antenna streams, and each stream provides 30 active subcarriers over a 20-packet temporal window. After amplitude and phase are concatenated, the transmitter dimension is omitted because only one transmitter is used, leaving a $3 \times 3 \times 20 \times 60$ tensor that is flattened into the 180 WiFi tokens consumed by the encoder in Section~\ref{sec:method}. Section~\ref{sec:dataset-acquisition} gives the recording protocol.

\subsection{Task Definition}
Given a CSI tensor from the fixed transmitter--receiver layout, the model produces 100 score-ranked pose queries, each represented by 14 3D keypoints. Following Person-in-WiFi 3D, evaluation uses Hungarian assignment between the query set and Kinect-derived ground-truth poses. The target is therefore low pose error together with reliable coverage of people under the fixed-layout protocol.

\subsection{Motion-Aware Spectral Tokenization}
The baseline maps each 60-dimensional CSI feature to a 256-dimensional token. The spectral tokenizer preserves this linear path as a residual bypass and reshapes the resulting 180 tokens into nine spatial groups with 20 temporal steps each. For every group, a depthwise temporal Conv1D (kernel size 5) followed by GELU smooths local variation. A real FFT along the 20-step axis produces an 11-bin magnitude profile, which a two-layer MLP maps to 20 sigmoid gating weights. The gated temporal features are channel-projected, added to the linear tokens, and normalized with LayerNorm. This branch is motion-aware feature modulation after projection, rather than a direct Doppler estimator.

\subsection{Flattened Mamba-2 Sequence Encoder}
The flattened Mamba-2 sequence encoder replaces a Transformer-heavy encoder with a compact sequence model. In the selected configuration, tokens are processed as a single WiFi sequence rather than through multiple manually routed branches. This reduces parameter count and latency while retaining sequence context across spatial and temporal CSI tokens.

\subsection{Lightweight Query-Pose Head and Flow Refinement}
The lightweight query-pose head predicts person hypotheses through 100 learned pose queries. During training, Hungarian assignment and the classification, keypoint, and bone losses are computed on draft predictions. For matched positives, the flow module receives a detached 42-dimensional draft pose, the target pose, and a 256-dimensional query condition. The training objective follows a conditional velocity-matching form:
\begin{equation}
x_t = (1-t)x_0 + t x_1,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
v^*(x_t,t,c)=x_1-x_0,
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{flow}= \|v_\theta(x_t,t,c)-v^*(x_t,t,c)\|_2^2.
\end{equation}
The draft is perturbed with Gaussian noise of standard deviation 0.1 during flow training, and $t$ is sampled uniformly from $[0,1]$. The velocity network concatenates the 42-dimensional pose, the 256-dimensional query condition, and a 64-dimensional sinusoidal time embedding; it projects this 362-dimensional input to 512 dimensions, applies three residual MLP blocks, and predicts a 42-dimensional velocity. At inference, Euler integration updates the pose:
\begin{equation}
x_{k+1}=x_k+\Delta t\, v_\theta(x_k,t_k,c).
\end{equation}
The one-step and two-step settings use Euler steps of size $\Delta t=1$ and $\Delta t=0.5$, respectively, to report the final 3D keypoints.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
The selected Mamba-2 Flow (two steps) configuration uses 100 learned person queries, 256-dimensional embeddings, and 14 output keypoints. CSI is first mapped by a spectral \texttt{WifiInputAdapter}; the flattened Mamba-2 sequence encoder has three layers with $d_{state}{=}64$, $d_{conv}{=}4$, expansion 2, head dimension 64, dropout 0.1, a \texttt{time\_major} route, mean route fusion, no positional embedding, and no final attention block. The lightweight query-pose head applies 8-head query attention, a two-layer MLP draft regressor, and a binary classification head. The flow refiner is a conditional \texttt{VelocityMLP} over 3D keypoint coordinates and query-level features.

Training uses focal classification loss with weight 2.0, keypoint L1 loss with weight 5.0, bone-length loss with weight 2.0, and flow loss with weight 10.0 for the flow-refined variants. All configurations use AdamW with learning rate $2{\times}10^{-5}$ and weight decay $10^{-4}$. At evaluation time, predictions are assigned to ground truth with Hungarian matching under a 500\,mm threshold. Unmatched ground-truth persons receive a 500\,mm penalty in overall MPJPE; Matched MPJPE excludes this penalty and averages only over assigned prediction--ground-truth pairs.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\subsection{Dataset and Acquisition}
\label{sec:dataset-acquisition}
We use the publicly released Person-in-WiFi 3D dataset and acquisition protocol \cite{yan2024personwifi3d}. The original dataset paper reports ethics clearance for public release; this study collects no additional participant data. Four ThinkPad X201 laptops equipped with Intel 5300 WiFi adapters form one transmitter and three receivers. The transmitter operates on channel 128 at 5.64~GHz through a single antenna, while each receiver records three antenna streams. It sends 300 packets per second and retains 30 subcarriers per transmitter--receiver link. An Azure Kinect captures synchronized RGB-D observations at 15 frames per second. Each CSI block has size $1 \times 3 \times 3 \times 30 \times 20$ for transmitter, receiver, antenna, subcarrier, and time; concatenating amplitude with sanitized phase, removing the singleton transmitter axis, and reordering axes yields the $3 \times 3 \times 20 \times 60$ input and 180 tokens.

The dataset contains recordings from seven volunteers performing eight daily actions across an office, classroom, and corridor. Each 40-second clip contributes its first 550 frames to training and final 50 frames to testing, following the baseline protocol. Kinect SDK failures, including four-person cases, are excluded in the released benchmark; the resulting 1P/2P/3P split contains 89{,}946 training and 7{,}824 test samples, summarized in Table~\ref{tab:dataset-stats}.

\subsection{Training Setup}
\label{sec:training-setup}
All configurations use the same 20-epoch AdamW schedule with base learning rate $2{\times}10^{-5}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, batch size 32, deterministic seed 42, and FP32 precision. CSI inputs use discrete-wavelet amplitude denoising and phase normalization. Experiments run on an NVIDIA RTX 6000 Ada Generation GPU with PyTorch 2.1.0, CUDA 12.1, and cuDNN 8.9.2. Runtime is model-only \texttt{simple\_test} inference on a batch-one $3 \times 3 \times 20 \times 60$ tensor: preprocessing, data I/O, and evaluation matching are excluded. Each result uses 10 warm-up iterations followed by 100 synchronized FP32 measurements; latency is the mean, throughput its reciprocal, and memory is peak CUDA allocated memory.

\subsection{Research Questions}
The results are organized around three questions. RQ1 asks how representation, encoder, and compact pose-head choices affect the accuracy--efficiency trade-off. RQ2 asks how flow inference changes matched localization and missed-person behavior. RQ3 asks which configuration provides the most useful fixed-layout operating point under the shared protocol.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:eval-metrics}
For a predicted 3D pose $\hat{P}$ and the matched ground truth $P_{gt}$, the per-joint position error (PJPE) of joint $k$ is the Euclidean distance between predicted and ground-truth coordinates, and the mean per-joint position error (MPJPE) averages PJPE over the $K{=}14$ keypoints \cite{ionescu2014h36m}:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PJPE}(k) = \left\| \hat{P}_k - P_{gt,k} \right\|_2,\quad
    \mathrm{MPJPE} = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathrm{PJPE}(k).
    \label{eq:mpjpe}
\end{equation}
To diagnose where error concentrates, we additionally report the per-joint dimension localization error (PJDLE), defined as the $\ell_1$ distance along each spatial axis $a \in \{h,v,d\}$:
\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{PJDLE}_a(k) = \left| \hat{P}_{k,a} - P_{gt,k,a} \right|,\quad
    \mathrm{MPJDLE}_a = \frac{1}{K}\sum_{k=1}^{K} \mathrm{PJDLE}_a(k).
    \label{eq:mpjdle}
\end{equation}
For every frame, the 100 score-ranked queries are assigned to ground truth by Hungarian matching. Pairs above 500~mm are rejected; each unmatched ground-truth person contributes a 500~mm penalty to overall MPJPE. Thus, overall MPJPE combines matched-pose localization and missed-person coverage, whereas Matched MPJPE averages only accepted pairs. This inherited metric is used consistently for all variants; it is not a detection average-precision measure. Results are further separated by scene cardinality (1P/2P/3P).

\section{Discussion}
The ablation shows how a compact draft-to-refine design changes the WiFi pose-estimation trade-off. Mamba-2 Draft establishes the speed and memory advantages of the flattened Mamba-2 sequence path, while Mamba-2 Flow (two steps) adds lightweight correction to obtain the lowest MPJPE in the evaluated architecture ladder.

The design ladder also shows that encoder substitution alone is insufficient: the Mamba PETR and Mamba-2 PETR variants do not improve accuracy, whereas the flattened Mamba-2 sequence encoder becomes effective with the lightweight query-pose head and flow refinement. Mamba-2 Draft and Mamba-2 Flow (two steps) consequently serve complementary deployment goals: the former maximizes speed and memory efficiency, while the latter minimizes MPJPE within the measured trade-off.

The complementary diagnostics sharpen this result. Mamba-2 Draft attains fewer unmatched persons and higher throughput, whereas Mamba-2 Flow (two steps) improves overall and matched-only MPJPE. The appendix reports per-joint behavior and a baseline-compatible receiver-masking diagnostic for the intended three-receiver input.

\section{Evaluation Scope}
The controlled ablations use deterministic seed 42 under the fixed three-receiver Person-in-WiFi 3D protocol. The reported comparisons therefore characterize architecture trade-offs within this shared sensing layout and temporal split; they do not make cross-environment or random-initialization variance claims.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents a compact draft-to-refine WiFi 3D pose pipeline. Under the inherited Person-in-WiFi 3D protocol, Mamba-2 Flow (two steps) achieves 165.487 mm MPJPE, improves on PETR Reference by 7.053 mm, reaches 209.9 FPS, reduces parameters to 3.618M, and lowers peak allocated memory to 25.29 MB. The architecture ladder shows that a flattened Mamba-2 sequence encoder with lightweight two-step flow refinement delivers the lowest measured MPJPE while retaining substantial efficiency gains over the PETR reference.
